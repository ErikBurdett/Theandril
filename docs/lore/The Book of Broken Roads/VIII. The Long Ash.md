> An age is not dark because nothing happens. It is dark because no one is left whose business is to watch.

From the Ashfall to the first rekindled charters ran one hundred and thirty years and some — the span the hearthfolk call the Long Ash. It is the worst-attested age since the Unwritten World, for the plain reason that the watchers were dead, scattered, or, in one case, walking the ruined toll-roads with a pack of ledgers, writing down what remained. This chapter is thinner than those before it. I was one clerk, on foot, in a broken world. I got what I could.

## What the world became

- **The hearthlands.** The Failing's deepest wound was distance itself. With no stone to hold a stranger's word, the world's working size shrank to the range of a man's own reputation — a valley, a river-reach, a coast. The ruined waystations, squatting at every ford and pass, marked the borders of these shrunken worlds like headstones. Men settled *between* them, in the sheltered pockets, and the pockets grew inward. These are the hearthlands, and the reader lives in one.
- **The plague years.** Ash-lung rode the dirty sky through every land three harvests running, and returned each dry summer for a generation. The living learned again to burn their dead quickly, which — I record the fact without comment, and the reader who has followed me this far will hear the comment anyway — made the Grave-touched arts at once more feared and more employed than in any age before.
- **The road powers.** Between the hearthlands moved the age's true sovereigns: mercenary companies paid in grain and girls, salvage brotherhoods stripping the waystations of their lead and wax, ruin-cults who worshipped at the quiet stones, and the monster domains — I use the settled folk's word — of the high cold and the deep places, which spread into lands men had held for two thousand years and hold them yet. Maps of the age mark such country honestly: *theirs*.
- **The false rekindlings.** Four times in the Long Ash, to my own knowledge, someone raised a stone and claimed the Roads were returning. Three were frauds of the ordinary kind, selling attestation no lattice backed. The fourth — at Cold Ford itself, in RR 2389, led by a woman the crowds called the First Witness Come Again — I traveled four hundred miles to see. I sat before her stone three days and heard it attest nothing, and said so in writing, and her people broke my hands and burned my pack. Ledgers can be recopied; I had duplicates buried in three counties. Hands mend, in my condition, slowly. I include the entry because the reader should know the price of the vow this book is written under, and who has paid it.

## What endured

Not nothing. It is the clerk's duty to record the credits with the debits, and the Long Ash held credits. Seed-grain crossed the dry country in Synod caravans and was not poisoned. The Deepfen took in three waves of Reedfen refugees and drowned none of them, whatever the upstream ballads claim. In the burnt uplands, the families who would become the wardens of the Cinder March held the pass-forts against the road powers for four unpaid generations, because someone had held them in their grandmothers' time and no one had ordered them to stop. And in every hearthland, in every generation, without exception and without coordination, the folk kept two habits the Failing could not sever: they taught their children letters, and they swore their little bargains at the quiet stones anyway — *for the habit of the thing*, they said. I have come to believe — one more unclerkly sentence, and the last — that the habit of the thing is the load-bearing wall of the world, and that the Roads were only ever its monument.

> [!note]
> It was in these years the candles began appearing at my door, and the name Ledgerbone attached to me. A lich who pays for lodging in fair copies of lost title-deeds acquires, over a century, a reputation of a complicated kind. I have been stoned out of eleven towns and begged back into nine of them, always for the same reason: sooner or later, every hearthland needs to prove what its grandfathers actually owned.
