> Say it at the stone, or do not say it at all. — hearth proverb, older than the Roads

Before roads there were fords, and before nations there were hearths. The first order of the world was small and fierce: a longhouse, a well, a wall, a fire that must not go out. What the hearth peoples lacked in numbers they made up in *memory* — and memory, in Theandril, was never a private thing. It was spoken aloud, at stones, before witnesses. This chapter records how that habit became the bones of civilization.

## The oath culture

A bargain spoken at a waystone was held to bind in a way no whispered promise could. Whether the stones themselves listened was already disputed then — I ask the reader to grow accustomed to that sentence, for it recurs through this entire book like a debt that is never paid down. What is attested is the *practice*: grain-shares, marriages, borders, blood-prices, all sworn at the stones before the assembled dead of both parties, whose names were recited so they might attend. Oathbreaking was rare and its punishment was not death but **erasure** — the breaker's name struck from every recitation, so that no ancestor would know him and no stone would hold his word again. Men who would cheerfully hang preferred not to be erased.

From the oath culture rose the first arts of rule: tally-sticks, then marks, then true writing, which the hearth peoples always regarded as a lesser, portable form of the stone-spoken word. The Oath tradition of magic — bindings, lineage-rites, the coercive sanctities that would one day be so catastrophically abused — grew up alongside, tangled with the Spirit tradition's ancestor-craft and the Grave tradition's quiet offices. Magic in this age was parochial, hedge-taught, and held in awe. There were no colleges. There was the woman at the edge of the marsh, and you went to her politely.

## The Witnessry

In the year now reckoned RR 1, at the ford called Cold Ford on the river Sallow, the elders of nineteen hearth communities did a new thing: they sealed a stone **jointly**, binding not one bargain but a standing service — that the stone at Cold Ford would hold *any* lawful bargain brought before it, for any party, in exchange for a toll of grain and remembrance. They appointed keepers to sit the stone in rotation, take testimony, and cut the seals. These keepers were the first of the **Order of Witnesses**, my order, and the toll-station they built beside the ford was the first waystation.

The invention spread the way fire spreads in a dry year, because it answered the oldest problem of the scattered hearths: *how do you trust a stranger?* You do not. You trust the stone between you. Within three generations there were forty waystations; within ten, four hundred, each sealed to its neighbors so that a bargain cut at one could be attested at all. The paths between them wore deep with feet and hooves and were paved by subscription, and men began to call the whole — stones, stations, keepers, paths, and the invisible lattice of held bargains — by a single name: **the Witness Roads.**
