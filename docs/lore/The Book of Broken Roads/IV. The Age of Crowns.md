> Gold is a promise that has forgotten who made it. — attributed to the Widow of the Deepfen; the attribution is not attested

For fifteen centuries — call it RR 800 to the eve of the Reckoning — the world was large, loud, and ruled. Five great powers dominated the attested lands, with a hundred lesser crowns, free cities, monster domains, mercenary hosts, and cult enclaves in the seams between them. I served the Roads through the last golden century of this age and its whole rotten aftermath, and I knew the great powers as a clerk knows his richest clients: by their seals, their debts, and their lies.

## The five powers

### The Iron Covenant

The deep-folk holds of the Anvilheights and their human client valleys, bound by the Kiln faith into one disciplined estate. Peerless in metallurgy, masonry, and the Rune and Stone traditions; their artificers' sealed work — self-turning locks, ever-burning forges, the great null-anchors of later infamy — commanded any price. Weak in Dream and Verdancy, and proud of the weakness: *a folk that does not dream does not lie to itself.* They said that. They believed it. Remember it when you reach the Reckoning.

### The Saltwind League

The charter fleets of the Outer Isles and the long coasts — not a kingdom but a lattice of shipping compacts, as the Roads were a lattice of stones. Masters of Tide, Storm, and Star craft, of navigation and of *paper*: the League invented the pledged cargo, the insured keel, the oath-debt sold to a third party. By the late age a League charter was itself a currency, and the High Charter of the Anchorage was crowned in everything but name. Their last High Charter, **Aldery, fourth of that name, the Debt-Crowned**, mortgaged three generations of unborn oaths to fund a trade war, and the stones held the pledge, because the stones held everything. The reader sees where this book is going.

### The Mire Courts

The elder courts of the great southern wetland, the Deepfen — fen-folk older than their own records, governing by season, ceremony, and patience. Deep in Verdancy, Spirit, and Dream; disdainful of heavy works; unconquerable at home, indifferent abroad. The Courts joined the Roads late, used them sparingly, and withdrew from them — I record the fact, and the year, RR 2289, and note that of all the powers of the old world, only the Mire Courts withdrew *before* the catastrophe. The folk of the Reedfen margin, who would one day be the Reedbound Council, were their tenants, tributaries, and unacknowledged children.

### The Sepulchral Synod

The priest-state of the southern chalklands, inheritors of the bone terraces, where the dead of forty generations were housed, ranked, and — their word — *employed*. Grave, Star, and Shadow were their estates; necromantic logistics their unmatched art. Synod caravans crossed deserts without water, Synod terraces raised harvests with tireless labor, and no plague, famine, or war ever produced in Synod lands the mass graves it produced elsewhere, because in Synod lands a grave was a beginning, not an end. Other realms traded with them eagerly and washed afterward. The Synod's hierarch, the **Provost of the Ninth Terrace**, was never in my long lifetime named, seen unveiled, or attested dead. It was the Synod that granted my petition, and I owe them my continuing, and I mark my debt here where every reader can see it and weigh my testimony accordingly.

### The Null Throne

The human kingdom of the Middle Reach — the old heart of the hearthlands, ruling from **Wardhall** over the river plains where Cold Ford itself stood. The Throne's founding grievance was magic itself: a dynasty raised on the wreckage of a sorcerers' war, sworn to the doctrine that the fourteen springs were fourteen leaks in the hull of the world. Modest in spellcraft by law; formidable beyond measure in Warding, engineering, and the arts of *nullification* — mage-hunters, ward-lines, and, in the last century, Covenant-forged null-anchors that could silence a working across a whole valley. The Throne guarded the greatest density of waystations in the world and took its tolls gravely. When the reader learns, in [[VI. The Reckoning]], who first cut a waystation dead on purpose, the reader will already have guessed.

## The rot under the gold

The crowned age died of its own bookkeeping, and I watched the entries accumulate. Set these four side by side, in the order the stones met them:

1. **Oath-debt.** Bargains pledged against bargains, pledges sold, the sold pledges pledged again. By RR 2250 the lattice held perhaps forty times more promised value than the world contained. No one intended this. Everyone profited by it. The Witnessry registered it all, neutrally, as was our oath, and our neutrality was the load-bearing wall of the whole tower.
2. **Soul collateral.** It began in the Synod's lawful practice — labor of the housed dead, pledged like any other yield — and spread as fashion among the desperate and the vile everywhere: remembrance itself put up as security. A man's recitation, his standing with his own dead, forfeit on default. The Oath and Grave traditions twisted together into instruments the first hearth peoples would have burned us all for. The stones held these pledges too. They held anything lawfully sworn. That was the whole of their virtue, and the whole of the horror.
3. **Counterfeit seals.** In RR 2263 a Veilcraft working surfaced in the Anchorage that could dress a false bargain in a true seal. It was suppressed, its makers hanged in their own rigging at the Anchorage Feast — the League fed its guests first; they were civilized — and it surfaced again, and again, for it was too useful to die. Every forged seal that passed attested was a coal laid against the day the whole ledger would be asked to prove itself.
4. **The mortgaging of the Witnessry.** Last and worst, and I record it against my own order. Stations took standing pensions from crowns. Chapters ruled on disputes their patrons had interests in. We remained honest in the small and were purchased in the large, and we told ourselves the lattice was sound because the *stones* were sound. First Recorder **Maude of Grey Weir**, my teacher, was hanged in RR 2229 by a League factor's court for refusing to backdate a seal. The order's protest was filed, registered, and attested at four hundred stones. No one was punished. That was the Age of Crowns, entire, in one entry.

> [!warning]
> I have been accused of writing this chapter coldly. I sat the stone at Grey Weir while the age described here mortgaged the world. Cold is what I have instead of screaming.
