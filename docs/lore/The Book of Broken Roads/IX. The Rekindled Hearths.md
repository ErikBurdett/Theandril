> Keep the hearth. Keep the oath. — the whole of the Ashen Compact's founding charter that anyone bothers to quote; the rest is grain-schedules, and I have read the rest

In the last two generations the Long Ash has ended — not by proclamation but by symptom. Roads are being paved by subscription again. Charters are being sealed that strangers honor. Wayfinders are mapping past the ruined stations, and the maps *sell*. Four powers of the new kind now stand in the lands this book has walked, and I set them down as they stand in RR 2447, with their virtues, their quarrels, and their inheritances from everything recorded above. They are small, by the measure of [[IV. The Age of Crowns]]. So were the nineteen communities at Cold Ford.

## The Ashen Compact

**Banner: hearth-gold. Word: *Keep the hearth. Keep the oath.***  The Compact is what the displaced made of displacement: the craft households burned out of the old upland towns in the Ashfall, knitted together over four generations by public oaths sworn in open square — for they trust no sealed room, and having read this book the reader knows why. Its hearth councils prize the reliable workshop above all things; their cinder-masons' reusable kiln-forms are the quiet marvel of the age, and a Compact journeyman's mark on a tool is the nearest thing the hearthlands have to attested value. Their standing quarrel is grain: how much of the common store the frontier settlements may draw before they have proven out. It is argued in council, publicly, interminably, and — I record this with something a mortal writer would be permitted to call affection — *honestly*. They are the only power in this chapter that has never once asked me to alter a record. They ask me to witness them instead.

## The Reedbound Council

**Banner: reed-green. Word: *No river belongs to one shore.***  The wetland villages of the Reedfen margin, bound by seasonal assemblies as their fen-court landlords bound them in elder days — the form is pure Mire Courts, and the Council would sooner drown than admit it. Between assemblies the Council barely exists; in assembly season it is the shrewdest single body of bargainers in the hearthlands, for these are folk who survived the Failing's refugee years on their neighbors' terms and buried the memory shallow, where it works. Their standing quarrel is drainage: the upstream landholders cut channels and the downstream boat families lose the deep water they live by, and every assembly ends with the matter tabled, because the Council's true constitution is the shared knowledge that the fen outlasts everyone who tries to own it outright.

## The Cinder March

**Banner: burnt-rose. Word: *We hold what the fire spared.***  The pass-fort families of the burnt uplands, hardened by four unpaid generations into a hereditary wardenship, now paid at last — and there is the quarrel. The mines under the fells have reopened, and the miners' coin finances walls the wardens once held on habit alone, and men who finance a thing eventually ask why they do not direct it. Warden and mine-master need each other, resent the needing, and both arm accordingly. The March is the most fortified, most disciplined, and poorest of the four powers, and its shield-drill — formations trained to cover one another as the old Throne infantry never learned to — is studied by every captain in the hearthlands. If war comes to the rekindled lands, whichever side has the March holds the high country; every power in this chapter knows it; the March knows they know. That is a whole politics, and I have set it down in one sentence, and the reader may have the sentence free.

## The Glass Tide

**Banner: sea-grey. Word: *Every horizon is a promise.***  The charter towns of the vitrified coast, who took their name from the Ashfall's glass and their habits from the drowned League — charters, shares, pledged keels, the whole grammar of the old paper sea, rebuilt small and cautious by folk who pick their ancestors' catastrophe off the beach every morning. The Tide's standing quarrel is its own founding question: the old routes past the coast are dangerous — glass-shoals, road powers, and worse things attested farther out — and reopening each one is a public work that every town wants opened and no town wants to fund. Their assemblies are auctions of blame. And yet the routes *do* reopen, one by one, because the Tide holds the League's true inheritance, which was never the fleets: it is the conviction, bred deeper than grief, that the horizon is owed to them.

> [!warning]
> Beyond the four: the mountains hold and do not answer; the terraces endure and do not explain; the Deepfen watches its former tenants prosper with whatever the fen-courts use for feelings; the road powers still rule the wide ruin between the hearthlands; and the monster domains have had a hundred and thirty years of unwatched increase. The rekindled lands are a clearing in a very old forest. I say so not to darken the reader's hope but to size it honestly, which is what hope is for.
